Four things worth guessing first

Each one asks you to commit to an answer before it runs. Guessing wrong is the useful part — that is the moment the explanation has something to fix.

Why the tooth count is the only number that matters

Every gear has a pitch circle: the circle where it would touch its partner if both were plain friction wheels. Teeth are a way of guaranteeing that this imaginary rolling happens exactly, with no slip and no creep, and nothing else. Turn on pitch circles and you will see the two dashed circles kiss at a single point, right in the middle of the mesh.

Because the pitch circles roll together, the rim speed of both gears is the same, so ω₁r₁ = ω₂r₂. And since teeth on meshing gears must be the same size, the radius is just the tooth count times a constant — the module, millimetres of pitch circle diameter per tooth. The constant cancels, and you are left with ω₁N₁ = ω₂N₂. Speed down by N₂/N₁, torque up by the same factor, minus friction. Nothing about the shape of a tooth gets a say.

That is also why an idler is free: it divides and then multiplies by its own tooth count, and the two cancel. It earns its keep by bridging a gap between shafts and by flipping the direction, not by changing the ratio. Each mesh reverses the direction, so a train of gears alternates: count the meshes, and an odd count comes out backwards.

Why one planetary set has three different ratios

A planetary set has three ways in and out — sun, ring and carrier, the arm the planets are pinned to. Hold any one of them still and the other two become an input and an output with a different ratio each time. One lump of metal, three gearboxes. That is why they are inside almost every automatic transmission, cordless drill and e-bike hub.

The whole thing obeys one equation, Willis's: Nsωs + Nrωr = (Ns+Nr)ωc. Set one ω to zero and read off the other two. Ring held: reduction 1 + Nr/Ns, same direction. Sun held: reduction 1 + Ns/Nr, same direction, much gentler. Carrier held: the planets are nothing but idlers and you get −Ns/Nr — a speed increase, backwards. Notice the planets never appear. They set the geometry, not the ratio.

They do set what fits together, though. Ring teeth are forced to be Nr = Ns + 2Np, because the planets have to span the gap, and you can only space planets evenly if Ns+Nr divides by their number. Drag the two sliders and watch that readout refuse some combinations.

These are real gears, and what is still a lie

The tooth flanks here are genuine involutes of a circle — the curve a taut string traces as you unwind it off a spool. Involute teeth have a property nothing else does: they transmit rotation at a perfectly constant ratio even if the two shafts are not quite the right distance apart. Push the centres a little wide and the gears still run smoothly, just with more backlash. Every other curve rumbles.

Everything is drawn at the textbook proportions: 20° pressure angle, addendum one module, dedendum 1.25 modules. At the module you pick, the SVG sheet comes out 1:1 with a 50 mm bar to check your printer against — the outlines are real enough to cut or print, though you will want to add backlash and a root fillet before you trust them in something that matters.

The lie: below about 17 teeth a real 20° cutter gouges into the flank near the root — undercut — and the drawn tooth here does not show it. The small gears on this page are a little stronger and a little quieter than their real counterparts. The ratios are honest, which is the part this page is about.
