How to play

  • Tap a siding and the loco drives in, pushing whatever it is hauling, and couples up to every wagon standing there.
  • Tap the last wagon you want to keep (or the loco itself to leave everything) — the loco uncouples behind it and pulls back into the headshunt. The green ✂ marks show the legal places to split.
  • The headshunt holds the loco and only 3 wagons. Siding 1 holds 5, sidings 2 and 3 hold 3 each. Anything longer fouls the points.
  • One trip into a siding and back counts as one move. Keyboard: 1 2 3 pick a siding, then 0–3 wagons to pull out.

Where it comes from

Inglenook Sidings is a classic of British railway modelling, devised by Alan Wright: a tiny shelf layout that is still fun to operate for hours, because the rules make every card a little logistics problem. Real yard crews solve the same thing every night, with longer trains and less patience.

How the solver works

Only the wagons on the card matter; the other three are interchangeable. That leaves about 356,000 possible arrangements of the Classic yard. When the page loads it runs a breadth-first search backwards from every finished train, so it knows the exact number of moves left from any position you can reach. That number is the "shortest" score, and the hints simply pick a move that brings it one step closer. Every card is checked to be solvable before you see it.
