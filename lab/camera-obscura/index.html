Camera Obscura Lab

A room, a wall, and one small hole. Light from the world outside streams through the hole in straight lines and paints a real, upside-down image on the far wall — no lens required. This is the oldest camera there is. Drag the sliders and watch why it works the way it does.

Why it's upside down

Light leaves every point of the object in every direction, but only one ray from each point happens to be aimed exactly at the hole. A ray from the top of the object travels downward through the hole and lands on the bottom of the screen — and vice versa. Every point crosses through the same single hole, so the whole image flips.

Sharp vs. bright

A tiny hole only lets through one narrow ray bundle per object point, so each point stays a tight point on the screen — sharp, but dim. Widen the hole and many overlapping ray bundles get through, so the image brightens but each point smears into a disc, blurring the whole picture. Shrink it too far, past a few tenths of a millimetre, and diffraction blurs it again anyway.

A room-sized idea

The effect was described by Mozi in China around the 5th century BCE and by Aristotle not long after, studied rigorously by Ibn al-Haytham around 1000 CE, and later used by painters as a literal darkened room to trace a scene in correct perspective — camera obscura is Latin for exactly that: "dark room." Add a lens to sharpen and brighten the image at the same time, and a few centuries later you get a photographic camera — camera is the same word, just not dark anymore.
